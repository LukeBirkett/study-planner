By Maximilian Schich ([Schich _et al._ 2014](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1240064))

The authors set to model a network of cultural centres connected by the birth and death of more than 150,000 notable individuals to reveal human mobility patterns and cultural attraction dynamics.

This analysis was able to identify cultural hubs through emergent migration patterns. Patterns of growth per country and city varied across the 2000 year period but distribution of birth-to-death distances remained unchanged over more than eight centuries.

The reason this was such a good project for network science is the because the true underlying processes determining cultural history are "a product of complex interactions among large numbers of individuals, determined by difficult-to-quantify historical conditions." Network science allows us to construct a simplified model which focuses on the output manifestation of cultural hubs (and feeder location). 

The data used comprised the birth and death locations of 150,000 notable individuals aggregated over time.

In the first figure the authors demonstrated a repertoire of visualisations. 

D and E were themselves networks with D being a time filtered representation of the 18th contrary and E being location highlighting all migration for Europe. The latter used PageRank to for the size of the nodes. 

C was a scattergraph which represented hubs as birth or death detractors. This therefore took information created by the graph and visualised it is different, more direct way. 

A and B were inspections of the underlying data sources. They plotted the number of deaths over time, as well as, a heat map demonstrating the age of death over time.
